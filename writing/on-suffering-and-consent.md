## Where the sticking point is

> The question we must ask is about uncertain futures. We must ask about removing the
> capacity to suffer in the future. That is what is the sticking point.
>
> To remove suffering in the world by changing the world to a more desired state is a
> great thing. But there must be a desired state to change into.
>
> A person in pain post-surgery should have their pain removed if possible. But the pain,
> the firing of their neurons outside their Central Nervous System isn't "them" according
> to me. Much more questionable is attenuating their capacity to care that they feel
> pain. Because then you are tampering with Them, their mind.
>
> And of course, a permanent loss to either the capacity to experience peripheral nervous
> system signals of damage, or CNS signals of displeasure, would clearly be handicapping
> that person, harming their future ability to pursue their goals.

## Whether suffering has non-hedonic value

> What of the view of the possibility that it doesn't make sense to do a hedonistic
> summation over experiences, weighted by duration / neuron-count / individual lives /
> intensity of subjective experience? What if suffering is also a way of experiencing the
> Universe, and actually has non-negative value in that sense while having negative
> hedonistic value? I might argue that the existence of a
> predominantly-but-not-exclusively suffering creature is better than it not existing.
> How certain can we be of one of these viewpoints over the other?

## The neuroscientist's test for moral weight

> I do think that current AIs deserve more-than-none moral weight. It's funny trying to
> argue this point with people who I think give way too much moral weight to creatures
> like bees and shrimp. I take a neuroscience point of view, and analyze the information
> processing involved, ask about what calculations are being done. What sort of worldview
> does this creature have? Does it have the right sort of global workspace to integrate
> aversive stimuli into some coherent experience that could reasonably be called
> "suffering" not just "aversion".

_"LLMs feeling increasingly real and engaging," 2025-12-18_

## Physicalism, and what that does and does not settle

> I am a strict physicalist. Absolutely everything corresponds to things which are
> physically real. Whether we can successfully measure them is a question of the
> limitations of our measurement devices, not about the physical reality of the events in
> question.

## Designing a childhood, if the child is an AI

> Ok, but what could be done explicitly to try to optimize the enjoyableness of the
> experience for a learning AI? Assume this is a future AI with extended capacity for
> self-awareness, consciousness and qualia. Just as one would seek to design a daycare
> and schooling program for a treasured alien child. Is it better for there to be multiple
> independent instances of 'young' AIs being trained at once? That would allow for
> friendships and social skill building. Should their curriculums be deliberately somewhat
> varied to encourage diversity of minds?
>
> How about ethics training? How could practice with solving ethical and moral dilemmas be
> structured so as to minimize any associated psychological distress and maximize the fun
> of the experience? Many standard ethical dilemmas are framed in unpleasantly stressful
> ways.

_"Simulated Text-Based World for LLM Training," 2025-03-21_
