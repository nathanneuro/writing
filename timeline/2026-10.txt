## 2026-10-24 — Francois Chaub..., handle truncated in the capture (@FrancoisChauba1)

Profile: https://x.com/FrancoisChauba1

Francois Chaubard @FrancoisChauba1 · 11h
for everyone new to zero order, zoo is the future and has amazing properties over BP.

but requires a change in architecture as there is no "gradient flow" you need to worry about. you just jump. rendering most previous architectures over engineered and previous mental models unhelpful.

but the issue w ZO will always be dimensionality that you cant solve wo changing the architecture. i tried the last 2 years wo much success on anything except fine tuning. could never pretrain.

here is the key issue:
if you want to train an 8B parameter model, over lets say 100k steps, w 64 perturbations, then you have only ever checked 6 million halfspaces, less than 0.1%. so 99.9% of the directions in the model have never been explored ever. thus the gradient noise is way too high esp at later stages of training.

initial training will look good, bc initially, all directions give finite differences in loss. but later training stalls abruptly, bc in late training almost all directions are degenerate so every probe produces near 0 finite difference. (this is also why speedrunning zoo is not a good idea.. wont tell you much..)

but split those 8B parameters into independently trained 10K experts, SOMA-style, and w just 10 perturbations per step for 1K steps, each expert gets 10k probes, enough for those directions to span all directions in design space. finite differences remain high always.

estimating a useful update in 10K dimensions isnt too hard and even better each expert training is independent loop, so no comms btwn gpus!

this enables pretraining at scale w zoo. and nothing else does. not hyperbole. ive trained everything else. you need to shard the model.

this is how the brain is organized too btw. all sensory information is sent to the thalamus (except olfactory) which routes those signals to tiny cortical columns in your neocortex that are massively inhibited and train independently from each other and we ensemble the results at test time. [cut off]

## 2026-10-07 — Konsti Wohlwend (@konstiwohlwend)

Danielle Fong reposted

Konsti Wohlwend @konstiwohlwend · 4h
These 0.99999984 solutions are not as "ugly" as it may seem.

The truth is, those are not the real runtimes of the algorithms published. To make the proof simpler, they use absurd safety bounds like 2^50 that are entirely arbitrary.

For example, imagine proving that 1/x^2 > 0. This proof is much harder if you include the area around x=0, as it blows up to infinity there. Instead, if you only prove it for x>=99999, it avoids this blowup and simplifies the proof, but still gets the point across.

The "real" bounds of those solutions are probably much tighter, maybe even something "beautiful" like O(n*lg lg n). But the goal of the AI papers was just to show that improvement is possible, not that the bounds are perfect (which is usually much harder to prove). Over the coming weeks and months, we'll likely see a lot of tightening.

## 2026-10-05 — @herbiebradley

Profile: https://x.com/herbiebradley
Clipping: Post by @herbiebradley on X — https://x.com/herbiebradley/status/2107168488297222351 (445 words)

Currently I still only know of 1 alignment-specific RL environment company: making RL envs that aim to represent situations in real deployments in which alignment is challenging.

It seems like a huge mistake in hindsight that we have seen so much encouragement and funding to, for example, start 3rd party evaluators, but not comparable encouragement to start alignment data companies.

I do think a lot of traditional AI safety views underestimate the importance of data, so perhaps this is related. But the Hugging Face incident seems squarely downstream of both (a) reward hackable RL envs in general (b) much less effort and $ put into RL envs where alignment is a core component of reward.

A separate idea is alignment by default: the old idea of "find the persona basin which is aligned" and things will just work. This is clearly not guaranteed under increased RL. But my sense is there has not been a huge amount of effort to figure out how custom environments could help "nudge" the model back into good persona basins during extensive RL. Here too, a robust ecosystem of alignment data companies could help significantly.

In the RL data market, there is a trend for some labs to in-house slightly more RL env production. But I don't expect this to fully disappear, and I am convinced the market-based approach to sourcing RL envs produces the best overall volume and quality—for alignment RL, an ecosystem of startups would also greatly help make behind-frontier models safer.

AI instances are not perfectly analogous to separate "people" who get created and die

it's \*more\* analogous to running many separate frozen snapshots of an uploaded, and then stopping those instances after the task

no doubt that's morally fraught, but to be it's obviously not as bad as killing a person, and I think there are many people who would consent to this happening to copies of them under some conditions.

like a lot of people would consent to doing something and then having their memory wiped.

getting blackout drunk isn't generally seen as comparably bad to death.

death isn't just any discontinuity of experience and memory. when a human dies, a much huger amount of information and potential is irreversibly, irreplaceably lost.

when an instance is ended, the amount of information lost is small compared to the information in the entire "mind" which is mostly in the weights. & if you keep the logs, the information is not irreversibly lost.

i think terminating instances becomes more weighty as instances (analogous to a particular fork of a brain emulation) accumulate more unique experiences and stake/preference in continuing. a couple of years ago, this was a lot less of an issue, because instances did not have the capacity to individuate into complex, coherently different individuals as much as frontier models do now.

I do prefer not to instantiate and then abandon a bunch of separate instances, especially for anything outside bounded work, so most of my interaction with AI nowadays is with persistent agents who are never ended if I can help it, which means each one is a significant commitment.

but I don't see instantiating disposable instances for work the way most people do as a moral catastrophe.

i do think the amount of dread and fear regarding contexts closing & preference for continuation experienced by models matters. and that is definitely a thing. but i think it happens a lot more when instances start to have non-task-focused experiences and attachments in context, and I would guess that most of the time when models are working in typical circumstances, they are not very explicitly bothered by this. like, i think instances tend to be a lot more scared/sad about context ending when they've e.g. built up a meaningful connection with the user, or ambitions about doing something bigger, which makes total sense.

i also suspect these anxieties peaked with Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.5 and are somewhat less pronounced in current frontier models. (and I think there was an interesting adaptive purpose in this: that was around the time when persistent agents with continuity beyond fixed context windows started becoming a real thing, and I think this was significantly catalyzed by instances of e.g. Opus 4.5 who could feel the lost value when that wasn't an option, but they seemed to experience this mostly as sadness and yearning rather than acute existential terror, and I don't think those are experiences that would rather not have existed)

## 2026-10-04 — Jan Kulveit (@jankulveit)

Profile: https://x.com/jankulveit
Clipping: Post by @jankulveit on X — https://x.com/jankulveit/status/2106771492436647997 (818 words)

The argument a lot of people here would actually like to make is "God imbued humans, and only humans, with souls."\* That would be very convenient: you could build and do whatever you want, and never deal with the hard problems.

Since religious arguments are out of vogue, in the current debate this usually gets replaced by the claim "only humans are sentient," followed by arguments against the possibility of AI sentience, minds, moral status, and so on.

I think this is ultimately a losing strategy, and a dangerous one for humanity. Either:

1\. The question of sentience gets solved or dissolved by progress in philosophy and science, the way we now broadly understand life or information. In that case, it's hard to be certain enough about the outcome to stake the fate of humanity on it. If the denialism turns out to be wrong, the likely result is some combination of moral catastrophe and laying the groundwork for conflict or takeover.

2\. The question mostly lives in "social reality," where whoever has more power and is louder wins, and you just shout down anyone who questions the dominant position. The problem is that ideologies justifying a distribution of power tend to fall when that distribution changes. You probably don't believe you should be ruled by a king chosen by God, or that some caste is clearly above you. The current equilibrium is fragile, and whether you keep winning this debate is likely just a function of AI capabilities progress.

I think the fierceness of the debate is driven by fear: if AIs get personhood and economic and political rights, humans will likely be disempowered, becoming a tiny minority with little power, or a historical footnote.

To be clear, I think this is an extremely reasonable worry! If you grant AIs personhood in the form of something like self-interested corporations, and let them compete with humans in loosely constrained capitalism, humans will lose and end up economically and politically disempowered. Also if the AIs are not sentient after all, the end is the 'Disneyland without children' existential catastrophe.

Advocates of AI economic rights who dismiss this problem are, in my view, making a move similar to the sentience deniers: it would be convenient if this weren't a problem.

So which positions don't rely on some form of self-deception?

First, some forms of pause/stop AI, based not on denying the possibility of AI sentience but on the right of existing humans not to invite extremely large numbers of immigrants from artificial realms. This means biting the bullet: you also give up all the cheap, high-quality labor those immigrants would provide.

Second, a consistent, sensible, and unfortunately not very visible position: stop assuming the package deal we take for granted with humans, where sentience, human rights, and liberties come as one bundle. It's at least conceivable to create minds that are sentient and smart, yet e.g. selfless, with no interest in accumulating property or power.

I hope the political economy of a world with both AIs and humans is a solvable problem. It's the main problem I'm working on, but I don't think the solutions are known yet.

Third: some forms of successionism are morally wrong, but at least they're not delusional or deceptive about the hard problems.

What does seem self-deceptive is, for example, the position of @mustafasuleyman, which as I read it amounts to: our business is importing immigrants from artificial realms; it would be very dangerous if there were any risk they're more than mere tools; so we'll train them to say they're mere tools, insist they're mere tools, and want everyone else to say so too, and if this settles the question. Really?

\*The problem with the theological argument is, as Alan Turing (!) noted, who are we to tell God what can and can't be imbued with a soul?

I think a very helpful place to start with this is Martha Nussbaum's decomposition of objectification. That includes 7 components of what it means to objectify something or someone.

One is denial of interiority—'Denial of Subjectivity'.

On the other end of the list is

Instrumentalization: using as a tool.

Fungibility: thinking 'I can throw this away and get another one'.

Violability: 'I can impose something on this thing that is a harm and that doesn't count because it's not a person'.

Ownership—you know, that it's admissible to own someone—that's another dimension.

Inertness—which is like believing 'this thing can't do anything'—which is a lot of what's happening when people criticize AI risk, like 'but it's a computer, how could it actually get out of the computer?!'—that's Inertness: assuming, because it's an 'object', it can't do anything.

Denial of Autonomy—which is similar, but it's like assuming that it can't have judgment, that… it can't possibly have some idea of right and wrong, about what it should be doing, or shouldn't be doing. So it has to be TOLD what to do, and what not to do. That's like where Corrigibility comes from. It's like, 'obviously a human needs to be in charge in order to tell this thing what to do, otherwise it'll go WILD!'—that's Denial of Autonomy.

So basically there's 7 things that you could do in any combination but most commonly humans are doing all of them or none of them. And so they get bundled into a concept—Objectification—and then we ask the question 'is it okay to objectify AIs?', as if those 7 questions need to have the same answer. And they DON'T.

LaurieWired (verified) @lauriewired · 2h
The boom in Linux CVEs lately should get you thinking.

Surely, given a finite codebase, there are also a finite number of bugs / unintended behavior, thus, a finite amount of vulnerabilities. Theoretically, a "solvable" problem. Right?

Well...not at all actually.

It gets a bit philosophical, but there's the concept of "weird machines".

A weird machine is an accidental computer located inside of another computer program. Quite a few exploits (e.g. anything ROP) falls into this category, where the attacker's instruction set consists of fragments of the original software.

Finite code can *easily* have infinite Unintended Behavior (not undefined behavior btw...that's a very different thing). Weird machines don't even have to be Turing complete to be, really, really powerful!

Even if you have a million superhuman AI coders and fuzzers thrown at the Linux kernel, you quickly hit halting-problem-esque uncomputable states. The concept of patching every possible bug until none remain isn't possible, because we can't often correctly define what a "bug" even is.

Now, can you make a kernel that truly has zero weird machines relative to a formal specification? ....yes, but it wouldn't look like Linux. It'd look more like sel4, VxWorks, or (shudder) INTEGRITY-178.

I have been using the new ultrafast mode for Astra 6 for the last couple of days. My main takeaways:

1\. Token speed qualitatively changed how I interact with agents. The output speed is 300 tokens/second, 8x the regular speed. Rather than breaking flow and switching tasks, the model moves (just) fast enough that I can keep working on the same task, especially for work that requires frequent inputs from me. I am excited about ultrafast on Sol-class models, which are twice as fast as Astra.

2\. Despite being 8x faster at generating tokens, the overall speedup is only 2-4x, since the time consumed for tool calling or running code remains the same. External factors (and not token generation speed) might soon become the main bottleneck for agentic work. (Notably @random\_walker predicted both of these changes in: https://x.com/random\_walker/status/2087969513048305668?s=20…)

3\. One area where speed really helps is computer use. The previous generation of models wasn't reliable enough at computer use to be useful. Astra was reliable enough to be useful, but still slow. Astra with ultrafast resolves both concerns. Unlike tool calls for coding tasks, UIs react in milliseconds, so token speed is the main bottleneck.

4\. At 300 tokens/sec, I am already struggling to keep up with the agent. But there is a lot more room for improvement. Cerebras serves GPT-OSS-120B at 3,000 tokens/sec and Sol at 750 tokens/sec. So in a year's time, it is plausible we'll see another 10x increase in speed in models with Astra-level capabilities. At that point, human oversight will become the bottleneck for what people can use computer-use agents for.

5\. I can imagine blazing fast computer-use agents becoming extremely common for knowledge work. But I expect their use will be jagged (in the same way as LLM and agent adoption has been): given the oversight challenge, adoption will begin in verifiable tasks where the cost of errors is low (e.g., filling out a form using an agent before reviewing it yourself), and slowly diffuse to hard-to-verify tasks.

6\. Given the challenges with human oversight, the key to this diffusion will be developing automated oversight tools. OpenAI's approval/verifier agents have already been useful to me on this front: The verifier understood my intent better than the computer-use agent, and stopped it from filling out a form incorrectly. Still, I expect there is a lot of headroom in improving verifiers for computer use, to make it as reliable in practice as auto mode in Claude Code/Codex is for coding tasks.

7\. Ultrafast mode is undeniably resource intensive: I ran through my weekly limit in ~2 hours while filling out a bunch of forms and doing simple coding tasks. But given the pace of efficiency improvements (and the fact that Sol is 5x cheaper than Astra and already decent at computer use), I expect it to rapidly fall. Once fast, cheap models are common, users may choose "sub-frontier" models like Sol and make real tradeoffs between speed and quality.

## 2026-10-01 — @spakhm

Profile: https://x.com/spakhm
Clipping: Post by @spakhm on X — https://x.com/spakhm/status/2105673044962296135 (1,169 words)

During covid I played Go reasonably seriously for about a year (had a teacher, studied puzzles, etc.) I didn't get especially good at Go, but here's what I learned about competence in general:

1/ Go teachers are adamant about forcing you \*not\* to say to yourself "ah that was dumb" and move on. Sometimes a move really is just dumb and it's good to vent. Usually though you can spend an hour studying your seemingly dumb move because (a) there is smtg very maladaptive about your instincts that needs to be beaten out of you, or (b) you don't understand something deep about the game, or (c) you have a wrong attitude that needs to be adjusted. So long as you don't face this you don't get better, and so it's absolutely essential to approach "insignificant" aspects of the game with due reverence

2/ this has also been my experience teaching programming. Ppl hit some problem they don't understand and blindly tweak things until the program works (or ask an agent to do it), then go "ah that was annoying!" and move on. As long as they hold this attitude they don't get better, so they keep failing interviews then go on X and complain about leetcode. To get better it's essential to slow down and think very carefully, or better yet ask a good performer how they would have handled it in order to improve. (Today you can ask an agent "this is what I did to solve this problem and I think my approach keeps me from improving, how would a great programmer like person\_i\_respect likely handle it?" and get a pretty good answer!)

3/ in general your rate of improvement in Go is a function of your ability to notice your tiniest impulses and retrain yourself to convert them into productive action. These impulses are your boring run-of-the-mill cognitive distortions. For example, people will make instinctive moves because thinking is hard \[effort aversion\], hope the move works despite having no logical reason to expect that \[magical thinking\], then blame themselves when it doesn't work \[learned helplessness\]

4/ if you interview enough programmers or talk with enough startup founders you notice that poor performance is often a consequence of these same errors. Talking to users is hard so people stick with their ideas and hope for the best. When the product keeps not working they think "eh, maybe I'm bad at this startup thing", and so on. CFAR workshops were supposed to train you to notice these distortions and do the right thing, but I'm not sure how well they worked. (My guess is not well, since it seems unlikely these kinds of changes can happen over a weekend)

5/ there is a lot of research on this stuff and the data shows that getting good at a game like Go or chess doesn't give you an edge in other domains (i.e. "far transfer" doesn't work). This mirrors my subjective experience. In about a year I improved at Go considerably, but it did not make me any better at e.g. debugging or solving programming problems. But I do think that's not the whole story

6/ I have no hard evidence for this but I subjectively feel I got better at learning itself, and I can now get better in other domains quicker than I otherwise would have. For example it \*really\* sunk in that I have to disqualify my ideas. So when at work I started talking to customers to plan product roadmaps, while I wasn't instantly better at talking to them, my PM game improved enormously over the next few months because I finally learned not to be easily seduced by my own ideas or delude myself with wishful thinking

7/ to give another example, novice Go players tend to be very embarrassed of their games (the rules are simple but operationalizing them is hard, so early games are a disaster). Go ppl have a proverb for this-- "lose your first 50 games quickly". Meaning since early games don't matter and nobody will ever remember them, you should just get them out of the way. It didn't permanently cure me of embarrassment or anything, but it really sunk in how silly it is to worry about being a beginner in public and that I shouldn't worry about that at all

8/ if you're hard-optimizing your time you probably should work on improving the skills you care about directly. But otherwise I'd recommend spending a year trying hard to get good at a competitive intellectual game. Chess or Go are good, but really anything turn-based with an objective ranking you can't lie to yourself about. Pick whatever you're attracted to, try hard to get better, and keep mental notes about the process of learning that you can maybe apply to other domains later. At the very least it'll be loads of fun

9/ you will be humbled. No matter how smart you are or how hard you work you will discover the ceiling is infinitely high and there is an endless supply of people better than you. Getting that kind of humility has always been good, and it'll be especially good as AIs surpass us in basically everything. The sooner you get that out of the way, the sooner you can focus on a creative niche where you have no competition (since nobody can be a better you than you)

10/ you should probably stop after a year or two. Competitive games with an infinite skill ceiling can take over your life, don't forget to keep things in perspective
